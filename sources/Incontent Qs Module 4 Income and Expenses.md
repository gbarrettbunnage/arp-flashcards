##### Lesson 1: IFRS 15 – identifying the contract with a customer



On 1 June 20X5 Wheatley Ltd (Wheatley) sold a computer system to a customer for the list price of £5,400, delivering it immediately. As part of its midsummer promotion, Wheatley also promised to provide the customer with technical support services for a period of 6 months, free of charge. Technical support services are usually charged at £100 per month.  



What revenue should Wheatley recognise in its year ended 30 September 20X5 in respect of the transaction?





a) £4,860





b) £5,220





c) £5,400





d) £5,700



The correct answer is b.



There are two performance obligations in the contract: the sale of a computer system and the provision of technical support services

The transaction price is £5,400 (being the contract price)

This is allocated to the two performance obligations on the basis of their stand-alone selling prices of £5,400 (for the computer system) and £600 (for 6 months’ technical support services):





£

Computer system	£5,400/(£5,400 + £600) x £5,400	4,860

Technical support services	£600/(£5,400 + £600) x £5,400	540





£4,860 is recognised as revenue when the computer system is provided to the customer on 1 June 20X5

£540 is recognised over the 6 months in which the services are provided

Therefore, revenue in the year ended 30 September 20X5 is £5,220 (£4,860 + (4/6months x £540))

Which of the following contracts are within the scope of IFRS 15?



A written contract to sell a specified volume of goods to a customer at a fixed price, payable 30 days after delivery of the goods. The selling entity will make a loss allowance for 2% of the amount receivable from the customer in line with its normal accounting policies.



A verbal contract to sell a non-current asset that is no longer required to another party at a fixed price, payable on delivery.





a) Neither contract is within the scope of IFRS 15





b) Only contract 1 is within the scope of IFRS 15





c) Only contract 2 is within the scope of IFRS 15





d) Both contracts are within the scope of IFRS 15



The correct answer is b.







Contract 1 is written, indicating approval. Both parties’ rights are clear, as are payment terms. The contract will result in an increase to the selling company’s cash flows and it is probable that consideration will be collected (if it were not, it is unlikely that the selling entity would have entered the contract). The fact that an allowance for receivables is recognised by the selling entity does not indicate that it is improbable that consideration will be collected.







Contract 2 is for the sale of a non-current asset rather than the transfer of goods or services. Therefore, this transaction does not give rise to revenue from a contract with a customer and is not within the scope of IFRS 15. 



Wharfeview plc (Wharfeview), which develops and sells commercial properties, enters a written contract with Grove Ltd (Grove) for the sale of a building for £1,420,000. The contract has been signed by both parties.



&#x20;



Grove intends to open a bar and restaurant in the building. The building is in a town in which new establishments face a high level of competition. Grove has no previous experience running bars and restaurants and has no other significant assets or sources of income.



&#x20;



Grove enters a long-term financing agreement with Wharfeview for the £1,420,000 consideration. Grove controls the building from the inception of the contract and intends to repay Wharfeview through income that it generates from the bar and restaurant.



&#x20;



The terms of the financing agreement are on a non-recourse basis, meaning that Wharfeview can repossess the property in the event of default but cannot claim further compensation. The cost of the building to Wharfeview was £950,000.







Required:



Explain, with reference to the criteria identified in the standard, whether the contract between Wharfeview and Grove is within the scope of IFRS 15.



Solution

The contract is within the scope of IFRS 15 if five criteria are met. The first four criteria are met:



There is a written contract in place, signed by both Wharfeview and Grove, which indicates the intention of both parties to fulfil the contract.

Grove has control over the building and therefore has the right to use it as it wishes. Wharfeview has the right to payment for the building under the financing agreement.

The payment terms are a long-term financing agreement and therefore the payment terms can be identified.

The contract has commercial substance as Wharfeview’s cash flows will increase in the future.

The fifth criterion requires that it is probable that Wharfeview will collect the consideration from Grove that it is contractually entitled to. Collection of the consideration is not probable because:



Grove intends to repay the loan with income generated from the restaurant; however, there is significant risk due to the high level of competition in the marketplace and Grove’s lack of experience;

Grove has no other assets or sources of income with which to repay the loan; and

Grove’s liability is limited due to the non-recourse basis (ie no compensation is payable to Wharfeview if Grove cannot afford repayments).

Since all five criteria are not met, IFRS 15 does not apply to this contract.

##### Lesson 3: IFRS 15 – Allocation of transaction price and revenue recognition



Margerison Mobiles Ltd (MM) entered into a contract with a customer on 1 July 20X5 to provide mobile network services for a period of 24 months for £40 a month. It also gave the customer a handset on 1 July 20X5, free of charge. The stand-alone selling price of the handset is £700 and MM provides network-only deals on a 24-month contract for £20 per month. MM has determined that the promises to provide a handset and provide network services are distinct.



&#x20;



What revenue should MM recognise on the provision of the handset?





a) Nil





b) £480





c) £569





d) £700



The correct answer is c.







The transaction price is £960 (24 months x £40)

The stand-alone selling price of the handset is £700 and the stand-alone selling price of the network services is £480 (24 months x £20)

The transaction price allocated to the handset is therefore £569 (£960 x 700/(700+480))



Beverley Landscapes Ltd (Beverley) entered into a contract with a customer on 30 September 20X3 to provide weekly gardening services over a 12-month (52-week) period, starting immediately. The total contract price is £18,000 and this amount is payable in equal amounts midway through the contract and at the end of the contract. Beverley’s year end is 31 December 20X3. There is one performance obligation in the contract, as Beverley is providing a series of services to its customer that are substantially the same and have the same pattern of transfer.



&#x20;



The transaction price is £18,000 and this is allocated to the single performance obligation.



&#x20;



The customer simultaneously receives and consumes the benefits of the gardening service as Beverley provides it, and so this is a performance obligation satisfied over time.



&#x20;



13 weeks of 52 weeks’ gardening services have been provided and therefore the performance obligation is 13/52 complete.

&#x20;



No consideration has been received from the customer yet and therefore a receivable is recorded.

b) Costs incurred as a proportion of total expected costs

Stubham Green Solutions Ltd (SGS) manufactures and sells heating systems to customers. It has entered into a contract to sell an energy-saving heat pump boiler system to a customer in the hospitality industry. The system was dispatched from SGS’ warehouse on 31 March 20X3 and was delivered to the customer’s hotel premises on 2 April 20X3. SGS used its own vehicle for distribution and was insured for damage to the boiler system during transit. On receipt of the boiler system, the customer provided an electronic signature on an electronic proof of delivery note (POD). The POD was instantaneously emailed to SGS’ sales department.







Required:



Advise, providing explanations, when SGS should recognise revenue for the sale of the boiler system. When you are ready, select ‘Solution’ to review the correct answer.



Solution

None of the criteria for revenue to be recognised over time are met and therefore it is recognised at a point in time.



&#x20;



SGS should recognise revenue when control of the boiler system transfers to the customer.



&#x20;



The boiler system was dispatched on 31 March 20X3; however, the customer did not receive the equipment until 2 April 20X3. Until 2 April 20X3, SGS still bore the risk of damage in transit (albeit a risk shared with the insurance company), and the customer did not have access to the rewards of using the asset, since it was still in SGS’ possession.



&#x20;



On delivery, possession of the system transferred to the customer and the customer accepted delivery by signing the POD.



&#x20;



From this point, the customer can access the benefits associated with directing the use of the asset and no other party is entitled to direct its use. From this date, the customer also bears the risks of holding the boiler system (eg a breakdown).



&#x20;



As the POD is returned as a signed document, a sales invoice can be raised on 2 April 20X3. This invoice creates an enforceable right to payment.



&#x20;



Therefore SGS should recognise revenue on 2 April 20X3.



Badger Ltd (Badger) sells items of machinery. In which of the following cases is there an indication that Badger should recognise revenue?



A machine has been shipped to Customer X, which is located in another country, and Customer X has been required to insure the machine against damage caused during transit.



Customer Z has taken delivery of a machine for a trial period and is not committed to making any payment until the trial period is complete and the customer has accepted the machine.





a) Both 1 and 2





b) 1 only





c) 2 only





d) Neither 1 nor 2



The correct answer is b.

##### Lesson 4: Revenue recognition scenarios



Imagine that a gym charges a £20 joining fee and a £50 monthly membership fee. 







Does the £20 joining fee relate to a separate performance obligation (step 2 of the five-step approach)?







When should it be recognised as revenue (step 5 of the five-step approach)? 



Solution

If the up-front fee relates to the transfer of a promised good or service, and that good or service is a distinct performance obligation, revenue should be recognised when the performance obligation is satisfied.

If the up-front fee does not relate to a distinct performance obligation, it is a prepayment for the goods or services provided throughout the contract period and should be recognised as revenue over that period.  



Northtown RFC (NRFC) charges new junior playing members a joining fee of £30 and annual subscriptions of £60. In exchange, the new members are able to access regular training and coaching sessions throughout the season (September to May). On joining the club, new junior members are provided with an NRFC hoodie and beanie hat.



&#x20;



Does the £30 joining fee relate to a separate performance obligation (step 2)? When should it be recognised as revenue (step 5 of the five-step approach)?



Solution

The provision of the hoodie and hat is a distinct performance obligation from the provision of training and coaching sessions since members can benefit from each separately and the two promises are separately identifiable.



&#x20;



Therefore, NRFC should recognise £30 revenue when a member joins the club and £60 over the 9-month period from September to May.

Denton is confident that if the customer requires delivery of its goods there will be sufficient in its refrigerator to meet the request.
